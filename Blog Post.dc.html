What is calibration?

When expressing the result of a forecast, one should accompany it with an assessment of how confident they are of this forecast, this is the task of uncertainty quantification. Different sources of uncertainty exist, from intrinsic aspects of the input data (measure imprecision, noise, stochasticity, etc.) to aspects in the methodology (imperfectly trained ML models, misspecified objectives, etc.). Knowing that these exist, it is easy to see why an estimation of how confident one can be is useful. However, this raises the question: how can we know that the quantified uncertainty itself is actually reliable?

We usually express this uncertainty by giving prediction intervals. We say that the ground truth is expected to lie within a range between two predicted values with a given level of confidence. Importantly, if we predict that the ground truth will lie within a given range with 95% confidence, we would like the ground truth to actually fall within that range 95% of the time. When the stated confidence levels correspond to the observed frequencies in this way, the forecast is said to be well calibrated.

If instead the true value falls in the predicted interval less often than 95% of the time, we say that the model is overconfident. Conversely, if the ground truth is contained within this range more often than expected, we say that it is underconfident

In probabilistic forecasting we don't just predict the extremes of the interval, but we aim to predict the probability distribution over the possible future outcomes (the predictive distribution). A prediction interval is simply a region of this predictive distribution containing a chosen fraction of its probability mass. Instead of checking calibration only at one confidence level, such as 95%, we can assess the predictive distribution as a whole using the probability integral transform (PIT).

Let FFF denote the cumulative distribution function of our predictive distribution, and yyy a ground truth observation IT=F(y)PIT = F(y)PIT=F(y)The PIT value of yyy measures the probability mass below it in the predictive distribution.If 70% of the observational data fall below y, and y has a PIT value of 0.7 under the predictive distribution, the model is well calibrated at that probability level. If this correspondence holds across all probability levels, the resulting PIT values are uniformly distributed over [0,1][0,1][0,1]. A flat histogram means the forecast is calibrated. A U-shape means it's underdispersed (overconfident): the predictive distribution is too narrow, so true values often land in the tails. A hump in the middle means it's overdispersed (underconfident): the predictive distribution is too wide. Asymmetry means the forecast is biased.



I hope this gave you an intuition for calibration. Calibration is an important aspect of evaluating probabilistic forecasts, but it is not the only one: two forecasts can have similar calibration scores while exhibiting very different predictive behavior. For example, one may be sharp but biased, while another may be well centered but overly dispersed. For this reason, calibration metrics should be reported alongside measures of accuracy and sharpness to provide a more complete evaluation of a probabilistic forecast.
